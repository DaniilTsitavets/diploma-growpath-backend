## Growing mint from seed or cuttings

Mint seeds are tiny and need light to germinate, so press them onto the surface of moist compost and barely cover them. Germination is slow and takes 10 to 16 days at about 18-21 °C. Cuttings are faster: a 10 cm stem tip put in water roots within one to two weeks and can then be potted up.

## Light

Mint grows well in full sun to partial shade. About 3 to 6 hours of sun a day is enough, and in hot summers some afternoon shade keeps the leaves soft and stops the soil drying out too fast. In deep shade the stems become thin and the flavour is weak.

## Watering mint

Mint likes constantly moist soil. Water as soon as the top 1 to 2 cm of soil start to dry, and never let the pot dry out completely because the leaves wilt and scorch. It still needs drainage holes: wet soil that never drains rots the roots and turns the lower leaves yellow.

## Containing the spread

Mint spreads fast through underground runners and will take over a bed. Grow it in its own pot, or sink a pot into the ground so the runners cannot escape. Keep different mints in separate pots. Trim runners that creep over the rim or out of the drainage holes, and do not stand the pot on bare soil where it can root through.

## Soil and feeding

Mint grows in any fertile, moisture-retaining potting mix. Feed lightly every 3 to 4 weeks in the growing season with a balanced liquid fertiliser; too much feed makes soft growth that attracts aphids and dilutes the flavour. Repot or divide a crowded plant every two to three years when the roots fill the pot.

## Harvesting and pruning

Pick leaves whenever you need them, cutting stems about 1 cm above a pair of leaves so the plant branches. Regular harvesting keeps the plant compact. After flowering, cut the whole plant back hard to a few centimetres; fresh, tender growth follows within weeks. Removing flower buds early keeps the leaves milder.

## Pests and diseases

Aphids and spider mites are the common pests; rinse them off and use insecticidal soap if needed. Mint rust shows as orange powdery spots under the leaves and later brown patches. Remove affected leaves and stems at once, throw them away rather than composting them, and avoid overcrowding. A severely infected plant should be cut to the ground and disposed of.

## Winter

Mint is a hardy perennial and survives frost. The stems die back in autumn and new shoots appear in spring. Cut dead stems down, keep the pot where it will not freeze solid or sit in water, and water only a little in winter. A pot kept indoors on a cool, bright windowsill keeps leaves for longer but grows slowly.
